Voting and polling places

Voters feel the welcome.

How we can help

Five ways in, from one site to the whole cycle

Five ways to work together, from a single site survey to ongoing advising. Each path stands on its own: start with one, or combine them across a cycle. Each one also lives inside the six-stage program below, and the last line of each card says where.

Path 1 · Site selection

Polling place accessibility surveys and site selection

Full site surveys during site selection, while a candidate or returning site can still be fixed, adjusted, or replaced. Each survey is done in person against ADA standards and accessible voting places guidance, and covers the full voter path: accessible parking and access aisles, the route to the entrance, doors and thresholds, the voting area layout, signage, and notice placement, including height and readability from a seated position.

Inside a clerk's operation, findings are documented with photos, plain language notes, and measurements where they matter. Nothing is left as a general impression, and a site that cannot be made ready is named as one while a replacement is still possible.

What you get: a written findings report for each site, with photos and fixes listed in priority order, so site selection decisions rest on what a voter will actually meet on the day.

What happens next: the surveys set the baseline for the readiness checks, and the fixes join the same sequenced list the program works through after the election. In the program: Stages 1 and 2.

Path 2 · Readiness

Readiness checks before doors open

A short, focused pass at each center before an election, and again on Election Day morning before doors open. This is the check that catches the problems a survey done months earlier cannot, because sites change: deliveries arrive, rooms get rearranged, and temporary setups drift.

Inside a clerk's operation, the pass confirms accessible parking and the accessible route are clear, the accessible voting machine is set up and positioned for privacy, and notice boards can be read from seated height in one horizontal band. It also confirms who owns accessible parking that day, and that cones, signs, and a temporary parking plan are on site if they are needed.

What you get: a same day fix list handed directly to the site supervisor, short enough to act on before voters arrive, with anything that cannot be fixed that morning logged for follow up.

What happens next: the check repeats at midday and in the late afternoon, when deliveries and vendor activity peak, and the fix lists feed Election Day problem solving and the post-election debrief. In the program: Stage 2, and the checks inside Stage 3.

Path 3 · Training

Election judge and poll worker training

Practical accessibility training for election judges and poll workers, built around real Election Day moments rather than rules in the abstract. Sessions run as a half day or a full day, in person or virtual, and the materials are left with your office for use in later trainings.

Inside a clerk's operation, topics include disability etiquette, how to offer help and how to accept a no, auxiliary aids and services, setting up and offering the accessible voting machine, the temporary accessible parking protocol, and what to do when a voter reports a barrier, so the report reaches the supervisor and is logged that day.

What you get: staff who are more confident on the day, a shared way of handling access questions, and fewer moments where a voter has to explain the same barrier to three different people.

What happens next: the training is tied to the sites you actually chose, and the reporting route it teaches is the one used on Election Day. In the program: the plan inside Stage 1, delivered as its own service, working into Stage 3.

Path 4 · Consulting and advising

Consulting and advising, the ongoing path

For questions that do not fit a single survey or training day, this practice can serve as a fractional ADA Coordinator advisor to your elections office, on call for access questions as they arise: the same ongoing path described on the coordinator support page, pointed at elections.

Inside a clerk's operation, that includes review of site selection criteria, review of vendor and contractor coordination terms for election days so accessible spaces and routes are protected in writing, and accessibility input on voter facing materials, signage, and notice layout before they are printed or posted. It also includes a standing second opinion when a new site, a new procedure, or a voter report raises an access question and the office wants an outside view before deciding.

What you get: one named advisor who knows your centers and your cycle, clear written input the office can act on or file, and steadier decisions across the year, not only in election week.

What happens next: over time, the advising builds into a simple accessibility self-evaluation and a phased fix plan across centers, so improvements are sequenced by impact and cost rather than handled one emergency at a time. In the program: it runs beside all six stages.

Path 5 · After the election

Post-election accessibility debrief

A structured review after the election, held once the results work has settled and while the experience is still fresh for staff and voters.

Inside a clerk's operation, voter reports, site supervisor notes, readiness fix lists, and any access incidents from the cycle are gathered in one place and reviewed together, without blame and with a focus on what the system can do differently. The review separates one off problems from patterns: a single blocked space versus a gap in contractor coordination that could recur at any center.

What you get: a short written summary with prioritized changes for the next cycle, owners and timing where you want them, and a record that lessons from one election carry into the next.

What happens next: the summary becomes the starting checklist for the next round of site selection, so the next cycle starts from evidence instead of starting over. In the program: Stage 5.

1. Pre-planning strategy

Decide access before you decide sites

Site selection criteria that screen for access before a site is committed, not after. Parking and passenger loading, the route from arrival to check in, entrances, interior routes, and space for an accessible voting booth are scored for every candidate site, alongside capacity and logistics, so access is a selection factor and not a retrofit.

  • Site selection scorecard for candidate polling places and voter service centers
  • Full accessibility surveys of candidate sites during site selection, done in person, so a site can be fixed, adjusted, or replaced before it is committed
  • Early voting, vote center, and precinct polling place models compared
  • Backup site planning when a first choice site changes late
  • Poll worker staffing and training plan tied to the sites you actually chose

2. Inspections and audits

Readiness inspections, site by site

Each site is walked the way a voter meets it: arrival, parking, route in, doors, check in, ballot marking, and exit. Temporary elements get the same attention as permanent ones, because a blocked accessible space, a propped sign, or a booth placed in a corner can undo a compliant building.

  • Pre-election readiness inspection for every site, with photos and measurements
  • Setup diagrams so the accessible booth, signage, and routes land in the right place on setup day
  • Temporary barrier check: queues, tables, cords, displays, and parked vehicles
  • Election Day morning check before doors open, repeated at midday: parking and route clear, machine set up and positioned for privacy, notices readable from seated height, and a named owner for accessible parking that day
  • Findings ranked by what blocks a voter completely, versus what slows them down

3. Election Day triage and monitoring

A named contact, a simple reporting route for poll workers, and a simple rule for barriers reported on the day: fix first, document as you go. What cannot be fixed on the day is logged with a photo and becomes the first item in remediation.

Inside your operation, that means a poll worker who finds a blocked accessible space knows exactly who to call, and the fix is attempted before the documentation, not after a voter has already been turned away.

4. Remediation

After the election, findings become an ordered list: quick corrections the county or building owner can do now, scheduled work, and big items scheduled with planned renovations. Title II entities add this to their transition plan.

What happens next: each item carries an owner and a date, so the next election cycle inherits a shorter list instead of the same findings photographed again.

5. Post-election debrief

Close the loop while the memory is fresh

A structured debrief with clerks, site leads, and poll workers: what blocked voters, what the fixes were, and what changes before the next election. The debrief becomes the baseline, so the next cycle starts from evidence instead of starting over. Inside a clerk's office, that is the difference between a binder nobody opens and a starting checklist the next site selection meeting actually uses.

6. Multi-site and multi-county management

One program, not a pile of site reports

For jurisdictions running many sites, findings come together in one view: sites inspected, sites ready, open barriers by severity, owners, and dates. The same method scales across counties that share vote centers, training, or equipment. Multi-site audit work is scoped by site count and travel. Fees are set by scope, audience, and format, and are agreed in a discovery call.
