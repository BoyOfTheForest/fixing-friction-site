WHAT HAPPENS WHEN FRICTION BUILDS

People do not leave all at once.

Burn Out: What a person feels

Burn out is what happens inside one person when daily friction uses up their capacity. They are still there, but they have nothing left to give.

Turn Out: What you start to see

Turn out is when people stop showing up before they quit. Meetings get thinner, volunteering dries up, and the people you count on go quiet.

Churn Out: What it costs you

Churn out is when people leave for good. You lose their skill, their relationships, and everything they knew, and you pay to replace all of it.

What ADAC means

ADAC is the Certified ADA Coordinator credential. It marks a coordinator trained to help an organization understand its ADA duties, coordinate access work across teams, and keep that work documented and moving.

What the ADA is

The Americans with Disabilities Act is the federal civil rights law that requires equal access in employment, public services, public accommodations, and telecommunications, so disabled people can take part on equal terms.

The method

TLCCW: Think, Learn, Communicate, Create, Work

The result names the stage. TLCCW is how you work on it.

TLCCW stands for Think, Learn, Communicate, Create, Work. It is a philosophy and a mindset before it is a method: a way of moving through the world when your brain or body works differently, whether you are on your own, with your family, out in the world, or at work. It was built for neurodivergent people, disabled people, and people living with chronic conditions, and for the leaders who shape the environments they move through. Five steps, in order, each one small enough to start today. Each step is shown here for a worker first, then for a leader.

Think

Name what is costing you the most.

For a worker

Start with the cost, not the verdict. The question is what is costing you the most, not what is wrong with you.

For a leader

Start with the cost across the team: where people go quiet, stop showing up, or leave. Act on the cause behind the numbers, not just the vacancy.

Learn

Find out what can be changed, and what the rules actually say.

For a worker

Find out what is in your control and what the rules say about it. The ADA Titles, in plain language, are a place to start.

For a leader

Find out what the standard requires and what your own findings show. A review reads a place against the standard and against lived reality.

Communicate

Say what you need in plain words.

For a worker

Name the barrier in plain words and ask for what would fix it. Your own words are the evidence: you are the one who lives with the barrier.

For a leader

Say it back in plain language. Findings are written in plain language, ranked by what blocks access most.

Create

Build one small fix that fits how you work.

For a worker

One small fix, not a life overhaul. Pick the change that fits how you work and make it usable.

For a leader

Turn the finding into one fix with an owner and a date. An item without an owner is a wish.

Work

Use it in a normal day, then adjust.

For a worker

Try the fix in an ordinary day. If it does not hold, adjust it and try again.

For a leader

Check the finished work against the original finding before it closes, and keep tracking the plan until the last item is done.

    The everyday moments it looks at

    Most team friction around disability is not a policy failure. It is a hundred small moments nobody was ever shown how to handle. The snapshot listens for them by name:

    • Microaggressions: the small comments and habits that single someone out, such as talking to a companion instead of the person, expressing surprise at ordinary competence, or making a diagnosis the topic of casual conversation.
    • Disability etiquette in daily interaction: ask before offering help, and accept no for an answer. Speak directly to the person. Never touch a person, a wheelchair, a cane, or any mobility aid without permission; an aid is part of that person’s space. Never pet, distract, or ask to separate a working service animal from its handler.
    • Language habits: how the team describes people day to day, including each person’s own preference for person first or identity first language, the assumptions inside common phrases, and inspiration framing that praises ordinary work as remarkable because a disabled person did it.
    • Disclosure and privacy: who decides what gets shared about a condition or an accommodation, and who does not. A diagnosis is that person’s to tell, not the team’s to pass along.
    • Meeting and communication habits: captions on by default, agendas and documents shared ahead in accessible formats, visuals described out loud, and questions asked in ways everyone in the room can answer.

    The questions it asks

    • Where does communication break down inside the team, and where does it break down with clients?
    • Which access and accommodation questions do staff answer in more than one way?
    • What supports have team members asked for, and what actually arrived?
    • Where do neurodivergent staff, or neurodivergent clients, meet friction that nobody has named yet?
    • Which small daily habits, comments, or assumptions are landing as slights, even when nobody means them that way?
    • Which mix of training and consulting would close the gaps the snapshot found?

    What each handoff actually does

    Training leads to Consulting and Advising

    A good session surfaces the real questions a team has been carrying: a policy that no longer fits, a grievance route nobody trusts, an accommodation call someone has to make this week. Advising settles those questions at the table, in what order, and why, before the work begins.

    Audits surface findings

    Decisions need evidence. An audit walks the building, the website, the polling place, or the stop the way a disabled visitor meets it, and writes down what blocks the way, photographed and ranked by what blocks access most. The walk runs at the level the question needs: a free Snapshot that offers one finding and opens the conversation, an Accessibility Assessment for one site, complete on its own, or a Full Accessibility Audit across the whole scope.

    Findings lead to Remediation support

    A findings list on its own does not move. Remediation support sorts the list into order, helps owners start, and keeps quick corrections, scheduled work, and capital work moving at the pace each kind of fix actually needs.

    Remediation matures into a Self-Evaluation and Transition Plan

    As fixes accumulate, the work needs a formal shape: an honest self-evaluation of programs and places, and a transition plan that phases what remains, with an owner and a target date on every item. For public entities, that plan is the written record leadership and the public can follow.

    Plans are carried, not filed

    Consulting and Ongoing Support works beside your team and your coordinator while the plan is carried out. For organizations that need steady help without a full time hire, the Fractional ADA Coordinator provides it inside ongoing support, on a three month minimum. Finished items are re-checked against the original finding before they close. For many clients, that carrying work is the ongoing engagement itself: training, advising, and consulting, month to month or quarter to quarter, shaped to the plan.

    Most access work fails between services, not inside them: training that never leads to a decision, an audit that never becomes a plan, a plan that nobody carries.     One row per Title. Covers in one line, then the core topics and keywords to scan for.

    Title I

    Employment

    Covers hiring and the workplace itself: applications, interviews, accommodations on the job, and the tools employees use. Reviews here look at the employee experience, from the job posting to the internal systems people work in every day. Digital findings often land here when the barrier is in a tool staff are required to use.

    Title II

    State and Local Government

    Covers everything a city, county, or state does for the public: buildings, programs, services, meetings, websites, and elections. This is where transition plans live, since public entities are expected to plan barrier removal over time. Voting and most municipal facility findings tag here.

    Title III

    Public Accommodations

    Covers private businesses and nonprofits that serve the public: hotels, restaurants, stores, venues, clinics, and schools among them. Reviews look at the full customer visit, from the parking lot to the checkout, including the website that booked the visit in the first place.

    Title IV is where the phone call lives.

    Captioning, relay services, and any process that assumes a person can simply pick up the phone and hear. Findings here surface in reviews of call based services, public announcements, and captioned media, and each one tags to Title IV the way every other finding tags to its Title.

    Title IV

    Telecommunications

    Covers telephone and communications access, including relay services and captioning for broadcast and public communications. Findings here show up in reviews of call based services, public announcements, captioned media, and any process that assumes a person can simply pick up the phone and hear.

    Inside an organization like yours, this is the support line that cannot be reached by relay, and the public announcement that was never captioned. What happens next: those processes are tested the way the people who rely on them actually use them, and the fix is verified on the same route.

    Title V

    Miscellaneous

    The provisions that hold the rest together, including protection from retaliation for exercising ADA rights and the relationship between the ADA and other laws. In the findings record, Title V is where findings about process are kept: how complaints are handled, whether people are safe raising a barrier, and whether a fix actually held.

    Inside an organization like yours, this is whether an employee or a resident can raise a barrier and trust the process that follows. What happens next: grievance handling is reviewed as part of the program, because a safe route to raise a barrier is what keeps the other four Titles working.

    How mapping works in practice

    One visit can produce findings under several Titles at once. A county office might have a Title II facilities finding, a Title II digital finding for its public website, and a Title I finding for the staff system behind the counter. The findings record keeps them tagged and separate for reporting, then brings them together in one transition plan with one timeline, so the client manages a single program of work instead of five disconnected lists.

    Title tags organize an assessment. They are a working structure for planning, not a legal determination of which Title applies in a given situation.

    How we do it

    Found. Named in plain words. Put in order. Fixed.

    A barrier is anything that slows a person down or shuts them out. The method is the same for a building, a website, a hiring process, or a polling place, and it runs in four steps:

    1. Look closely, in the place itself

    The place, site, or system is walked the way a disabled visitor, customer, or employee actually meets it, and every barrier is photographed and measured where it matters.

    2. Name it in plain words

    Each finding is written so the person who has to fix it can act on it without translation: what the barrier is, where it stands, whom it slows down or turns away, and the standard it touches.

    3. Put the fixes in order

    What blocks access most comes first. You get a phased plan with owners and dates, not a long list with no starting point.

    4. Fix it, then check it

    Finished work is checked against the original finding before it closes, and the plan is tracked until the last item is done.

    What is the ADA?

    The Americans with Disabilities Act is the federal civil rights law that requires equal access in employment, public services, public accommodations, and telecommunications, so disabled people can take part on equal terms.

    What is an ADA Coordinator, and what does ADAC mean?

    ADAC is the Certified ADA Coordinator credential. It marks a coordinator trained to help an organization understand its ADA duties, coordinate access work across teams, and keep that work documented and moving. An ADA Coordinator is the person who carries that coordinating role inside an organization. Matthew Cicanese, ADAC, brings three decades of lived experience to the work alongside the credential: the eye came first, and the credential formalized it.

    What are the key disability awareness days, and when are they?

    Five moments a year already put disability on board, HR, and communications calendars. April is Autism Acceptance Month. May is Mental Health Awareness Month, and it includes Global Accessibility Awareness Day. July is Disability Pride Month, with the ADA Anniversary on July 26. October is National Disability Employment Awareness Month, and it includes Invisible Disabilities Week. December 3 is the International Day of Persons with Disabilities. Awareness dates do not create the need. They create the deadline, so planning starts a quarter ahead. See the key dates and planning calendar.

    Five ways in, from one site to the whole cycle

    Five ways to work together, from a single site survey to ongoing advising. Each path stands on its own: start with one, or combine them across a cycle. Each one also lives inside the six-stage program below, and the last line of each card says where.

    Path 1 · Site selection

    Polling place accessibility surveys and site selection

    Full site surveys during site selection, while a candidate or returning site can still be fixed, adjusted, or replaced. Each survey is done in person against ADA standards and accessible voting places guidance, and covers the full voter path: accessible parking and access aisles, the route to the entrance, doors and thresholds, the voting area layout, signage, and notice placement, including height and readability from a seated position.

    Inside a clerk's operation, findings are documented with photos, plain language notes, and measurements where they matter. Nothing is left as a general impression, and a site that cannot be made ready is named as one while a replacement is still possible.

    What you get: a written findings report for each site, with photos and fixes listed in priority order, so site selection decisions rest on what a voter will actually meet on the day.

    What happens next: the surveys set the baseline for the readiness checks, and the fixes join the same sequenced list the program works through after the election. In the program: Stages 1 and 2.

    Path 2 · Readiness

    Readiness checks before doors open

    A short, focused pass at each center before an election, and again on Election Day morning before doors open. This is the check that catches the problems a survey done months earlier cannot, because sites change: deliveries arrive, rooms get rearranged, and temporary setups drift.

    Inside a clerk's operation, the pass confirms accessible parking and the accessible route are clear, the accessible voting machine is set up and positioned for privacy, and notice boards can be read from seated height in one horizontal band. It also confirms who owns accessible parking that day, and that cones, signs, and a temporary parking plan are on site if they are needed.

    What you get: a same day fix list handed directly to the site supervisor, short enough to act on before voters arrive, with anything that cannot be fixed that morning logged for follow up.

    What happens next: the check repeats at midday and in the late afternoon, when deliveries and vendor activity peak, and the fix lists feed Election Day problem solving and the post-election debrief. In the program: Stage 2, and the checks inside Stage 3.

    Path 3 · Training

    Election judge and poll worker training

    Practical accessibility training for election judges and poll workers, built around real Election Day moments rather than rules in the abstract. Sessions run as a half day or a full day, in person or virtual, and the materials are left with your office for use in later trainings.

    Inside a clerk's operation, topics include disability etiquette, how to offer help and how to accept a no, auxiliary aids and services, setting up and offering the accessible voting machine, the temporary accessible parking protocol, and what to do when a voter reports a barrier, so the report reaches the supervisor and is logged that day.

    What you get: staff who are more confident on the day, a shared way of handling access questions, and fewer moments where a voter has to explain the same barrier to three different people.

    What happens next: the training is tied to the sites you actually chose, and the reporting route it teaches is the one used on Election Day. In the program: the plan inside Stage 1, delivered as its own service, working into Stage 3.

    When a form, a doorway, a hiring process, or the tool stack itself was not built for them, people have to push harder, wait longer, and find a way around it. That daily drag is Accessibility Friction, and it builds in four places. Left standing, it does not stay in those four places. It compounds into three more: Legal Friction, Time Friction, and Bottom-line Friction.

    Start with an assessment
    Friction 1

    Digital Friction

    Websites, forms, portals, PDFs, and application processes. This is the friction the two web deadlines above are asking you to remove.

    Inside an organization like yours, it looks like a permit form that only works with a mouse, a PDF that a screen reader reads as a blank page, and an application portal that times out before assistive technology finishes it. Staff feel the drag as phone calls, walk-ins, and one-off workarounds.

    What happens next: the digital review lists the public services first, checks them against WCAG 2.1 AA and the Title II dates above, and puts the fixes in the same plan as everything else, so a redesign does not rebuild the old barriers.

    Friction 2

    Physical Friction

    Buildings, routes, parking, counters, signage, restrooms, meeting rooms, event spaces, and voting places. The barrier a visitor meets first is usually at arrival.

    Inside an organization like yours, it looks like a clinic entrance reached only by steps, a service counter with no lowered section, and signage that fades exactly where a first-time visitor has to decide. Each one turns a routine visit into a request for help.

    What happens next: the site is walked the way a visitor meets it, from arrival onward, photographed and measured where it matters, and the work is ordered so quick fixes happen now and big items are scheduled with renovations already planned.

    Friction 3

    Communications and Workplace Friction

    The Title I part: hiring, onboarding, employee rights and resources, the accommodation process, etiquette, support, and awareness inside the workplace.

    Inside an organization like yours, it looks like a job posting on a portal an applicant cannot complete, onboarding videos without captions, and an accommodation request that depends on one manager improvising well. The knowledge lives in people, not in a process, so it leaves when they do.

    What happens next: hiring, onboarding, the accommodation process, and employee resources are reviewed together. Managers get clear steps to follow, and training slows how fast new problems pile up.

    Friction 4

    Technology Friction

    The wrong software, or too much of it: too many overlapping tools, none of them quite right, costing time every day. Digital Friction is the form, portal, or process people cannot finish. Technology Friction is the tool stack itself, making every task harder.

    Inside an organization like yours, it looks like staff moving the same information between tools that do not talk to each other, re-entering what another system already holds, and working around software that works against the task instead of supporting it. For disabled staff the tax lands harder, because a wrong or bloated tool is one more thing to work around on top of the work itself, every day.

    What happens next: the software is reviewed as part of the friction picture. The wrong and duplicated tools are named, along with the time they waste, and the fixes are scheduled with everything else, so the daily drag stops building quietly in the background.

    When it is left standing

    Unaddressed, friction compounds.

    The four frictions above are where it starts. These three are what they become when issues are not addressed, remediated, and improved on over time. This is the part leadership tends to feel last, and pay for most.

    Friction 5

    Legal Friction

    What ADA non-compliance becomes over time: complaints, investigations, and lawsuits, including class actions, aimed at barriers that were visible, reported, or documented and still left standing.

    Inside an organization like yours, it looks like a barrier named in a grievance that is still there months later, a Title II deadline that passes with no plan in place, and the same fixable problem meeting the public again and again. At that point the penalties and filing figures above stop being someone else’s numbers.

    What happens next: an audit or assessment names the barriers while they are still yours to schedule. Remediation and a transition plan put the work in order, with owners and dates. That is a very different record from silence.

    Friction 6

    Time Friction

    The time Legal Friction pulls out of the week: leadership time, legal time, and staff time spent responding to a problem instead of doing the planned work that would have prevented it.

    Inside an organization like yours, it looks like emergency fixes made on someone else’s deadline, meetings called to answer a complaint, a launch paused to retrofit access, and the same questions returning because the first fix was never finished. Planned work waits while unplanned work takes the calendar.

    What happens next: fixes done in order take a fraction of the attention that fixes done under pressure take. Training and transition planning put the work on your schedule, in sequence, before a complaint puts it on a court’s.

    Friction 7

    Bottom-line Friction

    What executives see in the numbers: penalties, legal and settlement costs, remediation done in a rush at a premium, and the quieter loss of customers, residents, and employees who hit a barrier and never come back.

    Inside an organization like yours, it looks like paying more to fix the same barrier later, paying again when a rebuild recreates it, and losing the person who told no one and simply went elsewhere. The least expensive point to remove friction is before it reaches a budget line with a case name on it.

    What happens next: an assessment sizes the work and puts it in order, so leadership can fund the fix instead of the consequence. That is the conversation Bottom-line Friction is built to start.

    The prevention

    The work that stops the compounding

    None of the three frictions in this row has to happen. They grow in the space where an organization never gets the work that removes the first four.

    The solutions are the services of this practice: training that stops new friction being built, technology chosen and set up to work for everyone, audits and assessments that name what is standing, remediation that removes it, and transition planning that sequences the rest with owners and dates.

    What happens next: start with an assessment, and the conversation stays about fixes, order, and budget. Wait, and the same conversation happens later, with Legal Friction, Time Friction, and Bottom-line Friction already in the room.

    Where burnout lives

    PERP: personal, relational, professional

    Burnout does not respect the borders between the places a life happens. It is fed at work, carried home, and paid for in relationships, and it can be heaviest in a completely different place from where it shows. Matthew’s shorthand for the three places it lives is PERP: Personal, Relational, and Professional.

    Personal

    Your body, your brain

    Your body, your brain, your sleep, your executive function. The first squares on the board are almost always personal: starting gets hard, remembering gets patchy, rest stops restoring. This view teaches you to read yourself without shame and without spiraling into self-diagnosis.

    Relational

    The people carrying it with you

    Partners, family, households, teammates. Different people carry different weights in different areas at the same time, and burnout hides inside that mismatch: one person drowning in planning, another buried in physical chores, both convinced the other has it easier. This view makes both loads visible, together.

    Professional

    The work itself

    Workload, always-on availability, emotional labour, the meeting that could have been a message. This view separates a hard season from a system that cannot last, and gives you the words to take that difference into a real conversation at work.

    The board

    Burnout Bingo. Nobody wins. That is the point.

    The arc

    Four stages, one board

    1. Spot It

    The signs of burnout, named plainly, including the executive function signs most people misread as laziness or failure: starting, ordering, remembering, and switching tasks all going quiet at once.

    2. Catch It Early

    What to do at two and three squares, while the fixes are still small: hand something off, renegotiate, rest, and say the true sentence out loud before the row fills in.

    3. In the Thick of It

    Getting through burnout when you are already in it: smallest workable days, a plain sorting of what actually matters, what to say at work and at home, and recovery planned like a project, not a weekend.

    4. Prevent the Return

    Systems that hold when motivation does not: limits on how much you carry, early warning lines set while you are well, regular check-ins, and a board reviewed on a rhythm, so the next slide gets caught at the first square.

    Two truths at once

    Both can be completely true

    “I am carrying the planning, the remembering, and the emotional weather of this house” and “I am carrying the cleaning, the bills, and the repairs” can both be completely true, about the same home, in the same week. The same is true at work. Burnout conversations fail when they argue about whose truth wins. This is where the drawing of both starts.
